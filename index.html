How the model worksin plain words

It's a Monte Carlo simulation: the Korean War played out by chance 1,000,000 times, then counted. Every odd in it is our judgment, written down with what informed it: the official histories, the documents of the time and what historians have found in the Soviet and Chinese archives. No source gives these numbers, and nobody can measure them.

  1. One war runs month by month, from June 1950 to the end of 1955. It starts with the invasion of 25 June. Does the South Korean army hold north of Seoul? Does Truman send American forces (93% at the middle odds)? Then the summer: Osan, Taejon, the Pusan Perimeter, the two Naktong battles decide where the line stands, and MacArthur's Inchon landing (93% if he goes ahead) decides whether North Korea's army is caught or gets away. Nothing is forced: every choice reads the state of that war.
  2. Battles are events. 43 kinds of battle can happen, 29 of them fought in the real war: the invasion: Seoul, Osan (Task Force Smith), the Kum and Taejon, the Pusan Perimeter forms, the First Naktong Bulge, the Great Naktong Offensive, Inchon, the breakout and the KPA collapse, Pyongyang, Onjong, Unsan, air superiority south of the Ch'ongch'on, the Ch'ongch'on (Kunu-ri), Chosin, Hungnam, the Third Phase (Seoul lost), Twin Tunnels, Hoengsong, Chipyong-ni, Wonju, Killer and Ripper (Seoul retaken), the Spring Offensive, first impulse (Imjin, Kapyong), the Spring Offensive, second impulse (Soyang), the May-June counter-offensive, Bloody Ridge and Heartbreak Ridge, Commando (the Jamestown line), Black Thursday (MiG Alley), the outpost war (an active month), and Kumsong. Each is fought only if that war gets there. Its odd is the chance our sources give it at the force ratio it really had; a war in which the UN is stronger or weaker moves it, by the men on each side, their supply, their firepower and their cohesion. Then luck. The losses carry on: casualties, the line, the armies, American will and the date the war ends.
  3. The 38th, the warning and China. When the UN reaches the 38th parallel, Washington chooses: act on Zhou Enlai's warning of 3 October 1950 (20%), stop at the 38th, halt at the narrow neck between Pyongyang and Wonsan, or drive for the Yalu. China's entry is rolled by the road taken: 92% if American troops drive for the Yalu, 80% if they stop at the neck, 75% if they stop there with assurances to Peking, 45% if only South Koreans go north. A China that stays out can still come in later, 5% a month, while the UN holds the Yalu.
  4. The economy. America's troops arrive on the real schedule (178,000 US ground troops in Korea by 23 November 1950), then 22,000 replacements a month. The South Korean army is rebuilt with American money. The Chinese army in Korea grows to about 950,000 by April 1951 and 1,450,000 by December 1952, but the front it can fight with is capped by what its railways and porters can carry (2,400 tons a day in summer 1951) and by its trained men (80,000 a month). UN air attack cuts that supply; Soviet arms raise Chinese firepower; American supply thins out north of the 38th.
  5. Will and the talks. American will is the share of Americans who told Gallup the war was a mistake: it grows with casualties, jumps when China throws the UN back, and builds in a stalemate. It moves the big choices: evacuation, keeping MacArthur, a costly push. Talks open once Mao has turned to the defensive. An armistice is a chance each month: small while Stalin lives (2%), much larger after his death on 5 March 1953 (25%), which is a date in every war, not an odd.
  6. Escalation. Atomic bombs, bombing Manchuria's airfields, or keeping MacArthur and carrying the war to China can each make the allies leave and bring the Soviet Union in openly. An open Soviet entry ends the run as a wider war: the model does not play a war beyond Korea.
  7. Four leaders, four temperaments. In each war Truman, MacArthur, Mao and Stalin each get one temperament, drawn once. It pushes that man's choices a little bolder or calmer, never luck in a battle. Its average is the middle odds.
  8. One pass is one war. Then we count how each one ended.

What each ending means

  • Korea unified: The UN and the South hold all of Korea up to the Yalu for 12 months, with no Chinese army in Korea (China stayed out, or went home).
  • A better peace: The war ends with the line at or north of 39°N: the narrow neck, with Pyongyang and Wonsan on the UN side.
  • A draw near the 38th: The war ends between 37.90°N (about 30 km south of the real 1953 line at 38.17°N) and 39°N. The real war ended here.
  • A worse peace: The war ends south of 37.90°N: South Korea keeps less than it really did.
  • A loss: America fights, and the UN is driven off the peninsula or evacuates it.
  • A wider war: The Soviet Union enters openly: after American atomic bombs, after the bombing of Manchuria or MacArthur's war on China, or with ground forces to save North Korea. The model stops there: it does not play a war beyond Korea.
  • America stays out: Truman never sends American forces (or comes too late), and North Korea takes the South. Not counted in the shares: they are of the wars America fights.

A war still going at the end of 1955 with no armistice is judged by where its line stands. Pyongyang kept, the film's measure, counts Korea unified and a better peace together: every ending with Pyongyang on the UN side of the line. The shares are of the wars America fights (931,483 of the film's 1,000,000); in the other 68,517 America stays out.

Restarting from a date

To restart from, say, 24 November 1950, the model replays real history up to that date: every roll goes the way it really went (Truman sends troops, Inchon succeeds, the UN crosses the 38th, China comes in, Onjong and Unsan are lost). From that date on it runs free. Replayed all the way, it ends in a draw near the 38th in July 1953, every time: that's one of the tests.

Three things we want you to know

  1. A unified Korea needs China out, and that is an odd, not a result. Every one of the film's 47,311 unified Koreas is a war in which China stayed out or went home. With every big choice set as it really was and only the battles left free (300,000 wars), Korea is unified in 0.00% of them: battles alone never drive China out once it is in. China's odds come from the Chinese and Soviet documents (Chen Jian, Shen Zhihua, Donggil Kim), and historians still argue about them. Give China no choice at all and Korea is unified in 0.27% of wars.
  2. Once China was in, the battles decided how much of Korea America kept. Of the film's better peaces, rerun with one thing changed at a time, 87.6% are lost by turning one real battle the other way. Win every battle from Onjong on, with every choice as it really was (25,000 wars), and Pyongyang is kept in 92.8% of wars, but Korea is unified in only 3.9%.
  3. The atomic bomb is not a shortcut in the model. The Army found in June 1951 that "no suitable targets for atomic weapons in Korea were known": Chinese armies in mountains, not cities. So the bombs rarely break the front (10%), and they can bring the Soviet Union in (25%). That is our judgment, from the documents; it is the reason the film's atomic row ends in a wider war in 27% of wars.

The same answer on every computer

Each war's dice come from the seed and the war's number, nothing else, and the model does its own arithmetic for exp and log so every browser gets the same digits. War 420 of seed 19500625 is the same war on any computer, however many cores run it and in whatever order. The war numbers are the code's own: war 420 is war(oddsFor('mid'), rng(warSeed(19500625, 420))), and the first war of a run is war 0.

The ones viewers ask about most. For each: what really happened, from the sources behind the model, and what the model does with it.

Could China have been kept out?

RealOn 3 October 1950, ten past midnight, Zhou Enlai told India's ambassador, K. M. Panikkar, that if UN forces crossed the 38th parallel China would send troops; "this action would not take place if only South Koreans crossed" (FRUS 1950 VII, doc. 589). In Washington most "were inclined to regard it as a bluff", and the CIA judged intervention "not probable in 1950". At Wake Island on 15 October MacArthur put the chance at "Very little."

The modelThis is the film's answer. China's entry is rolled by the road the UN takes, from 45% if only South Koreans go north to 92% if American troops drive for the Yalu. Keep Americans at the 38th from early October and Korea is unified in 14.35% of wars, against 5.37% with nothing forced.

What would "winning" even mean?

RealThe Security Council's resolution of 27 June 1950 asked members to help South Korea "repel the armed attack and to restore international peace and security in the area". The General Assembly's resolution of 7 October 1950 went further, toward "a unified, independent and democratic Government" for all Korea. MacArthur's aim was China itself: "There is no substitute for victory."

The modelThe model scores all three. Saving the South is every ending but a worse peace or a loss; uniting Korea is "Korea unified"; beating China is not an ending the model offers, because carrying the war to China ends in a wider war in 29.98% of the film's MacArthur wars.

What if America had stayed out?

RealIn his speech of 12 January 1950 Dean Acheson drew America's "defensive perimeter" from the Aleutians to Japan and the Ryukyus, leaving Korea out. On 27 June Truman ordered "air and sea forces to give the Korean Government troops cover and support"; on 30 June he decided to commit ground forces.

The modelTruman sends forces in 93% of wars at the middle odds. Forced to stay out, America stays out of 97.0% of wars and the South falls; in the rest he comes late, to hold a pocket round Pusan.

Why could the UN act at all?

RealThe Soviet delegate had stayed away from the Security Council since January 1950 (over China's seat). Resolution 82 passed on 25 June "9 in favor to 0 opposed, with 1 member abstaining (Yugoslavia) and 1 member absent (U.S.S.R.)"; Resolution 84 put the forces under one American command on 7 July. Washington's own judgment: the prompt action "was due solely to absence of the Soviet" delegate.

The modelNot modelled: the war starts with the UN already able to act.

"You forgot us": the UN nations

RealSixteen UN members sent combat forces: the United States, Britain, Australia, the Netherlands, Canada, France, New Zealand, the Philippines, Turkey, Thailand, South Africa, Greece, Belgium, Luxembourg, Ethiopia and Colombia. Sweden and India sent hospital and field-ambulance units. By late 1950 the South Korean army was the largest: 223,950 men on 23 November 1950 against 178,464 Americans and about 20,899 others, and 590,911 at the armistice.

Inchon

RealMacArthur landed at Inchon on 15 September 1950 against the doubts of the Joint Chiefs and his own admiral. The tide that day reached 31.2 feet, "Only on this day" in that window. In two weeks North Korea's army came apart; only 25,000–30,000 got away north.

The modelThe plan goes ahead in 90% of wars and succeeds in 93%. Forced with no Inchon, Eighth Army breaks out alone, slower, and more North Koreans escape: Korea is unified in 4.40% of wars, against 5.00% with nothing forced.

Chosin

RealAt the Chosin Reservoir, X Corps was surrounded at twenty below zero and fought its way out from 27 November to 11 December 1950. When the Chinese blew the bridge at the pass, eight C-119s dropped bridge spans by parachute on 7 December; six were serviceable, "the only airdropped bridge in history" (Futrell).

The modelX Corps breaks out in 88% of the wars that reach Chosin. When it does, the Chinese IX Army Group is out of the front for 3 months; when it doesn't, about 20,000 American troops are gone for three months. X Corps is destroyed at Chosin in 5.9% of the film's wars.

Ridgway

RealWalker was killed on 23 December 1950 when his jeep hit a truck; Ridgway took command of Eighth Army on 26 December. He attacked again from 25 January 1951 (Thunderbolt), then Killer and Ripper; Seoul was taken back in mid-March, and Chipyong-ni, held in February, became the turning point. He succeeded MacArthur on 11 April 1951.

The modelNot as a man: the model has no commanders by name. Ridgway's war is in the 1951 battles (Twin Tunnels, Chipyong-ni, Wonju, Killer and Ripper, each its own event) and in the rule that the UN attacks only with enough of the force ratio it really had.

MacArthur, and the atomic bomb

RealMacArthur wanted Manchuria bombed, China's coast blockaded and Chiang Kai-shek's troops brought in: "There is no substitute for victory." Truman relieved him on 11 April 1951. Bradley called that strategy "the wrong war, at the wrong place, at the wrong time, and with the wrong enemy". On 30 November 1950 Truman had said "There has always been active consideration of its use"; an Army study in June 1951 found "no suitable targets for atomic weapons in Korea".

The modelKeep MacArthur in April 1951 and Korea is unified in 4.45% of wars, a wider war in 29.98%. Drop the bombs in the winter crisis and the war still ends near the 38th in 63.18%, a wider war in 26.61%.

The Soviet pilots

RealSoviet pilots first fought American aircraft over the Yalu on 1 November 1950, in North Korean markings and Chinese uniforms. Stalin kept them to the rear: "the Chinese rely only on their own aviation at the front". Their corps' own report claims 1,097 UN aircraft for 319 of its own lost.

The modelTheir covert air defence is in the model's air battle (the UN keeps air superiority in 93% of wars). Soviet aircraft flying openly over the front is an odd of its own (5%), and an open Soviet entry ends the run as a wider war.

China's view of the war

RealChina calls it the War to Resist America and Aid Korea. Xu Yan's count: 2.97 million Chinese troops went to Korea, and the war cost China 6.2 billion yuan, "only one-tenth of state spending", with 183,108 registered dead (2010). In August 1952 Zhou told Stalin that "Mao Zedong believes that the continuation of the war is advantageous to us".

The modelChina's choices are odds in the model: entering, attacking a halted line, going home (rare: 3% within a year with its army simply held at the neck). How China counts the war as a victory is not something the model scores.

The prisoners

RealFrom early 1952 the talks stalled over voluntary repatriation: only about 70,000 of over 170,000 held by the UN "would return to the Communists without the use of force". The United States "clung firmly to the concept for fifteen months", at a cost of "over 125,000 UNC casualties". In the 1953 exchanges 75,823 went north and 12,773 came back, 3,597 of them Americans; 21 American prisoners refused to go home.

The modelDropping voluntary repatriation is an odd (15%) that adds to the armistice odds each month. Forced, the endings barely move: Korea unified 4.98% against 5.00% with nothing forced, a better peace 11.21% against 11.03%.

Eisenhower and the end of the war

RealEisenhower promised "I shall go to Korea" on 24 October 1952 and went in December, with "no interest in a renewed ground offensive". In May 1953 Dulles told Nehru that America would make "a stronger rather than a lesser military exertion". Whether that mattered is disputed: Weathersby found "it was Stalin's death rather than U.S. threats to use nuclear weapons that finally brought a breakthrough".

The modelThe new president seeks an armistice in 75% of wars; the 1953 signals add 3% a month to the armistice odds. Stalin's death on 5 March 1953 is a date in every war, and after it the armistice odds jump from 2% to 25% a month.

The bombing of the North

RealUN bombers struck the North's towns from 1950 to 1953: at Sinuiju on 8 November 1950, 70 B-29s burned 60% of the built-up area; the fire raids on Pyongyang in January 1951 destroyed 35% of its built-up area; in June 1952 the power plants were knocked out. On 13 May 1953 F-84s broke the Toksan dam; the flood washed out miles of railway embankment and flooded an airfield. Estimates of civilian dead in the whole war run from 1.6 to 3 million (an encyclopedia range; the North never published a count).

The modelThe air war is in the model as supply cut from the Chinese front (UN interdiction, the dams, Manchuria); bombing the dams from August 1951 instead of May 1953 is an odd (5%). Civilian losses are not modelled.

Japan, the base

RealJapan was the war's base and workshop: in the first three months "practically all ammunition the U.N. and South Korean forces used came from rebuild stocks in Japan" (Appleman). Thirty-four Japanese vessels sailed for Inchon, and a Japanese minesweeper was sunk at Wonsan. MacArthur told Truman at Wake about the new National Police Reserve, the start of Japan's own forces.

The modelNot modelled: the war's economic effect on Japan (the "procurement boom") is outside the model and outside this film's research.

The weapons

RealNorth Korea attacked with 150 Soviet-built T-34 tanks; the South Korean army had no tanks, only 37-mm anti-tank guns and 2.36-inch bazookas. In November 1950 "as many as two-thirds of some infantry units lacked" small arms in the Chinese army (Mossman).

The modelIn the model, firepower is a number per man: North Korea's 1950 army at 0.9 of a UN soldier, a Chinese soldier at 0.45 in November 1950 rising to 0.9 by mid-1952 as Soviet arms arrive.

What it leaves outa wider war, civilians, politics…
  • A war beyond Korea. An open Soviet entry ends the run as "a wider war". What would follow, in Europe or anywhere else, is not played.
  • Commanders as people. MacArthur, Ridgway, Walker, Peng Dehuai are not in it by name; Truman, MacArthur, Mao and Stalin have one temperament each per war, and that's all.
  • Single units and the shape of the front. The front is one line, its average latitude across the peninsula. X Corps deep in the north-east in late 1950 is not drawn apart from Eighth Army.
  • Civilians. The model counts soldiers. The bombing of the North's cities, the massacres and the refugees are not in it; the North Korean and Chinese dead are rough, and calibrated only to wide ranges.
  • The prisoner war in the camps. Voluntary repatriation is one odd that can speed the armistice; the camps themselves are not modelled.
  • Politics at home. American will is one number (the Gallup "mistake" share); the 1952 election is one choice. Congress, McCarthy and the Chinese and Soviet leaderships' arguments are inside the odds.
  • What came before and after. How Korea came to be divided at the 38th parallel in 1945, Japan's postwar boom, the germ warfare accusation of 1952, and what Korea taught Washington before Vietnam are outside the model and were not part of this film's research, so the page says nothing about them it can't back.
How it was tested134 of 134 pass

The tests are part of the code you can download. They run the model and fail if it can't do what history did. The one that matters most: replay real history in full and the war ends in a draw near the 38th, in July 1953, every time, with the American dead within 5% of the real 33,739.

134 of 134 checks pass on the locked model. That shows the code isn't broken. It doesn't show the odds are right: they're our judgment.

Do the tests catch mistakes? To find out, 16 deliberate bugs were planted in copies of the model, one at a time, and the tests run on each: all 16 were caught. Among them: the twelve-month hold counted from the wrong month; a restart that ignores its date; dice that reshuffle when one event changes; Stalin's death switching the armistice on too early; the force ratio working backwards; China's entry rolled with the wrong road's odd.

Two review roundsand what they changed

Before the film's numbers were run, the model went through two review rounds, each a history review and a code review against the sources, done as AI-assisted review passes told to find what's wrong. Every fix went into the code, and each round added tests.

  1. Round 1. China's entry became a roll by road, with its own odds at the 38th, the neck and the Yalu. A unified Korea now has to hold twelve months against a late Chinese entry and a North Korean army rebuilt in Manchuria. The dice were keyed so that changing one event changes only what it causes. Atomic use stopped ending the war by definition. The headline moved to the wars America fights.
  2. Round 2. The history reviewer found that talks froze any line, so a line in Pyongyang no longer stops the Chinese army; that the neck road broke its own buffer; and that the model missed the Gallup numbers of 1951–52. Each was fixed and tested. A research round closed the gaps the reviewers named: the Soviet air corps' own report, the 1953 estimates, the ammunition limits of 1952.

One fix after the first full run. When a push to the neck ended, the model could set the line south of where the UN already stood. It was fixed, and every number on this page comes from the fixed model, run again in full: Korea unified 5.08% before and 5.08% after, a better peace 11.04% before and 11.09% after.

Then the model was locked (12 Nov 2026). The film's run used these files, whose sha256 fingerprints are below.

Questionsthe ones viewers ask
Is this really the model from the film?

Yes. The code is the same file, sha256 ac1d877846942b0f…. Run 1,000,000 wars at the middle odds with seed 19500625 and you get the film's 47,311 unified Koreas on any computer.

Where do the odds come from?

They're our judgment, written down with what informed each one: the US Army's and the Air Force's official histories, the Joint Chiefs' history, the State Department's documents (FRUS), the Soviet and Chinese documents published since the 1990s, the polls of the time. Every one is listed, with why. Think one is wrong? Change it in the simulator and run it.

Why is Korea unified so rarely?

Because a unified Korea needs China out, and in most wars China comes in. Once it is in, the battles decide where the line ends, not whether China leaves. The war ends near the 38th, where it really did, in 75.95% of the wars America fights.

What is "Pyongyang kept"?

Korea unified, or a better peace with the line on the narrow neck: every ending with Pyongyang on the UN side. 16.17% of the film's wars that America fights.

My run doesn't match the film exactly. Why?

A different seed changes the dice, and fewer wars is a smaller sample. The film's settings with seed 19500625 and 1,000,000 wars always give its exact numbers, and every button under Start from and What if loads the seed and number of wars the film used. Small differences between samples are chance, especially for rare endings.

What does "start from" do?

It replays real history up to that date exactly as it happened, then lets the model run free. It's how the film's gauge shows America's chance at each date.

Why does the first war have number 0?

The page uses the code's own numbers, so the film's war 420 is war 420 here and in the code: warSeed(19500625, 420).

Does the page send my settings anywhere?

No. The wars run in your browser, and your odds stay there. The page counts visits and each press of Run, with the name of the what-if button if you used one, using GoatCounter, which uses no cookies and collects no personal data. It never counts your odds, seed or results, and it strips the settings from the address before counting a visit. Its fonts load from Google Fonts, which sees an ordinary web request.

Is it a forecast?

No. It's a way to see what follows from the odds, and the odds are our judgment.
